The Junco and the Fire Engine

What two alarms in one neighborhood teach us about the stories we tell

Get new notes by email ↓

The lead story

At a certain point in life, bird watching stops being a hobby and becomes the evening news. In our neighborhood, the lead story is the junco. Juncos are beautiful, but it’s their song that gets the headlines: it sounds exactly like a car alarm.

“There’s the neighborhood alarm,” Jen and I will say when they arrive. For a second, we’re transported back to the hood, where such occurrences were commonplace. That’s the part worth noticing.

The junco doesn’t fool us because we’re careless. It fools us because it sounds exactly like something we’ve learned to take seriously. It borrows the authority of a real signal. You brace, you turn toward the street, and then it’s just a bird.

How folklore lands

Okay, that’s a little hyperbolic, but that’s how folklore works. It doesn’t fall flat. It lands, and often it lands hard, because it sounds like the accumulated wisdom of the field. It comes from someone with years in the work, it fits what the room already believes, and it has been repeated so often that nobody thinks to ask what’s behind it.

The response it produces is real: the nods, the conviction, the sense of having learned something. What’s missing is the thing it sounds like. The bracing is genuine. The alarm isn’t.

The other alarm

The same neighborhood sees a lot of medical emergencies. Fire engines pull in with sirens and horns. When we hear those, we know it’s a real emergency and first responders are on the way to deal with it.

Two alarms, one street. The junco borrows an urgency it hasn’t earned. The fire engine carries urgency with someone already on the way. Nobody on our street needs the difference explained.

The bracing is genuine. The alarm isn’t.

When the alarm stops working

Nobody on our street runs to the window when a car alarm goes off anymore. Car alarms trained us. They sounded urgent so many times, with nothing behind them, that we learned to stop listening. The junco is funny because we already tune out the thing it imitates.

That’s the hidden cost of a folklore story. Each one spends a little of the room’s urgency and returns nothing for it. Do it often enough and the room learns the pattern: the setup, the turn, the nods, and then nothing to do. When a story finally arrives with something real behind it, it lands in a room that has learned to sit still.

The fire engine only works because the siren still means something. Spend the siren on birds, and eventually it stops meaning anything at all.

You knew this was coming

Every fundraiser repeats it: stories are the lifeline of fundraising. That’s true. But a full week of conference stories tested the claim and came up empty. Story after story, nods all around, and the attendees walked out with nothing they could do on Monday. The actionable association was never delivered. The “so what” was missing.

Same form, opposite effect

A story can move the mission forward: it shows a donor what their gift did, or hands a practitioner something they can actually go do. Or a story can simply function as folklore: emotionally satisfying, it gets nods in the room, and it leaves people with nothing to act on.

What the research says about stories

Stories do work on audiences, and the receipts are real. Psychologists Melanie Green and Timothy Brock showed in 2000 that the more a listener is “transported” into a story, the more likely they are to adopt the beliefs it carries (Green & Brock, 2000). A 2014 meta-analysis pooling 76 papers confirmed it: transportation reliably moves beliefs, attitudes and intentions toward the story (van Laer et al., 2014). Closer to our field, Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic found people gave more to one named girl, Rokia, than to statistics about millions facing famine (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic, 2007).

Stories also pull listener and teller into alignment. Princeton neuroscientists Greg Stephens, Lauren Silbert and Uri Hasson scanned the brain of a woman as she told a story about her freshman year of high school, then scanned eleven people listening to a recording of it. The listeners’ brain activity came to mirror hers, mostly a second or two behind and in some areas slightly ahead, as if anticipating where she was going. The coupling vanished when communication failed, and the listeners who understood the story best showed the strongest coupling, especially in the areas that ran ahead (Stephens, Silbert & Hasson, 2010). A good story, in other words, brings the room into step with the person at the front of it. Whether that alignment leads anywhere depends on what the teller hands them once they arrive.

But notice what the research measures: belief, attention and giving in the moment. None of it asks whether the listener walks away knowing what to do next. And the story evidence needs its own receipts check. A 2024 replication of the Rokia study, run online with hypothetical donations, found no support for the effect; its authors call for a test with real money before the question is settled (Maier, Wong & Feldman, 2024). And the popular claim that stories are “22 times more memorable” than facts traces to no verified study at all. When Shawn Callahan of Anecdote went looking for its source, he found an estimate attributed to Jerome Bruner, not an experiment (Callahan, 2015). Even the folklore about stories is folklore.

The test

After the warm feeling fades, what can the listener do differently? If the answer is nothing, it was folklore wearing a story’s clothes.

The next time a story gets nods in the room, listen for what comes after. If someone is on the way, it was a siren. If not, it was a junco.

Addendum · October 16, 2026

On a second reading of the research, one line above claims more than the evidence does. “Even the folklore about stories is folklore” puts two different things in the same basket. The “22 times more memorable” claim is folklore: it traces to an estimate, not an experiment. The Rokia finding is not. One online replication using hypothetical donations did not find the effect, and its authors call for a real-money test before the question is settled. That makes the finding contested, not debunked, and a reason to hold it more loosely than our field usually does. The research on stories is real. Some of what we repeat about it is not.
